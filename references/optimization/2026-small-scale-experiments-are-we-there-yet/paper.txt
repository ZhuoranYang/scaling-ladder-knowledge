                                         1   Introduction
                                         Foundation models have revolutionized the way we think about computing. They arose as a paradigm only
                                         five years ago (Bommasani et al., 2021), yet in that time have become the basis for new discoveries across a
                                         broad set of scientific fields (Jumper et al., 2021; Lam et al., 2023; Merchant et al., 2023; Trinh et al., 2024;
                                         Hubert et al., 2026). As you might expect, those five years saw a flurry of research into how best to build these
                                         models, and one key ingredient emerged: scale. Unfortunately, scale makes experiments incredibly expensive.
                                         The natural solution is to experiment at the small scale, then transfer to the large scales. Despite the early
                                         promise of scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020), this solution has not yet materialized after half a decade. Instead,
                                         pretraining research grew exponentially in cost, with experiments (not the final training run) dominating the
                                         overall expense of model development (Cottier et al., 2025; Denain and Wu, 2026).
                                         Scaling laws failed to deliver because they hit a major roadblock, which is that they are notoriously elusive at
                                         small scales. Famously, the two most influential works in the literature reached contradictory conclusions.
                                         Kaplan et al. (2020) held parameters should scale much faster than data, as a result early language models
                                         were under-trained (Brown et al., 2020; Rae et al., 2022; Chowdhery et al., 2023); Hoffmann et al. (2022)
                                         found parameters and data should scale at the same rate. Porian et al. (2024) traced this discrepancy to
                                         methodological choices such as whether to count embeddings in the parameters, how long to warm up the
                                         learning rate, and scale-dependent hyperparameter tuning; choices that disproportionately affect small-scale
                                         models (Pearce and Song, 2024). This early discord foreshadowed later difficulty. Surveying the literature,
                                         Li et al. (2025c) found many scaling laws hard to reproduce and, in particular, that scaling laws based on
                                         smaller models with 100M parameters or less are unreliable. Li et al. (2025a) aptly summarize the situation
                                         as the “scaling gap where insights from small-scale experiments often fail to transfer to resource-intensive


                                                                                                  1
production systems”.
How do we close this gap and deliver on scaling laws’ original promise? We find that the recipe for a scaling
law has one key ingredient that, more than any other, determines final quality: rigorous hyperparameter
tuning (§3). By ablating different choices, we show scaling laws are fairly robust to details like how to count
parameters or whether to decay the learning rate (§3.1). However, poorly tuned hyperparameters can be
disastrous (§3.2). Whereas common practice tunes over a small grid, you may need to search hundreds of
configurations to reveal the scaling law in small models. Such a search sounds expensive, yet we also find
scaling laws extend remarkably far down; as little as 4M parameters where models train in under an hour on
a single GPU, bringing the cost well within reach for many academic and industry labs.
Hyperparameters unduly affect small-scale laws because small-scale models are unduly difficult to tune. We
explain this difficulty in terms of the hyperparameter loss surface (§4). As parameters and data grow together,
they drive good configurations to fill a larger fraction of the space and the intrinsic dimension of the surface
goes down (§4.1—§4.2). Moreveover, as model size increases, the effective number of hyperparameters drops
to one. This phenomenon introduces a new way of thinking about scaling, where one should do a thorough
job sweeping hyperparameters in the smaller scales to due to their sensitivity whereas larger models are
easier to tune (§4.3). Luckily smaller scale experiments are significantly cheaper to run than their larger scale
counterparts so proper scaling becomes a matter of proper computational budget allocation.
Building on our insights, we propose a new methodology for proper scaling drawing on three tools from
the literature (§5). Namely, the noisy quadratic limit reveals if hyperparameters are fully tuned and how
hard that is to accomplish (Lourie et al., 2025a); scaling laws describe how loss changes as models scale up
(Rosenfeld et al., 2020; Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022); and the perplexity–capability correspondence
answers whether conclusions about pretraining loss have consequences for the capabilities we ultimately care
about (Mayilvahanan et al., 2025).1 Small-scale experiments exploit strong assumptions and we argue for
an approach where each assumption becomes a diagnostic that improves our qualitative understanding of
how the model scales (5.1). This qualitative understanding is important because even though phenomena
like scaling laws exist at the small scale, quantitative techniques such as extrapolating the loss compound
small errors (5.2). Finally, we apply our takeaways to a question that once took the field years to settle
(5.3): where normalization layers should go in the transformer architecture, and find that larger scales favor
pre-normalization (Vaswani et al., 2017; Baevski and Auli, 2018; Xiong et al., 2020).
In the end, these ideas give a practical methodology for model-centric research, which improves the model
holding data fixed in contrast to data-centric research, which improves the data holding the model fixed. The
methodology is as follows: first build a complete understanding at the small scale by thoroughly exploring the
model’s hyperparameters, then use simple rules to carry it up. This regimen offers the rare opportunity to cut
costs and raise rigor, but it takes the right tools discussed in this work. With them, small-scale experiments
can finally deliver on their long-awaited promise. We note finally that our discussion focuses on model-centric
research while data-centric research likely requires different techniques (§6).


2     Background and related work

2.1    Scaling Laws
Researchers have long sought to predict the outcomes of large-scale training from small-scale experiments.
Early work studied how models improve with more data (Cortes et al., 1993; Hestness et al., 2017; Sun et al.,
2017); later work, with the arrival of web-scale data, turned towards parameters and compute as scaling laws
became the primary tool for managing the rising cost of experiments (Rosenfeld et al., 2020; Kaplan et al.,
2020). After Kaplan et al. (2020) demonstrated that pretraining loss falls predictably over many orders of
magnitude for language models, similar scaling laws were soon discovered in many other domains (Henighan
et al., 2020; Jones, 2021; Cherti et al., 2023). Such scaling laws offered an enticing promise: study a model
cheaply at the small scale, then extrapolate to the large.

However, the promise of predictable large scale performance for fixed compute budget proved difficult to keep.
Pearce and Song (2024) found small-scale experiments led to discrepancies across major scaling law studies
(Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). More recently, Li et al. (2025c) surveyed the literature, found
scaling law estimates hard to reproduce, and reported those built from small models are unreliable. We revisit
this regime and reach a more optimistic conclusion: with rigorous tuning, scaling laws exist even at a few
million parameters though we must extrapolate them with care.

2.2   Hyperparameter Optimality
With the evidence presented in the previous section, we might develop the intuition that tuning hyperparamert-
ers is one of the most important problems in estimating accurate scaling laws. If that is true, how do we know
if hyperparameters are fully tuned? Lourie et al. (2025a) found that near the optima, hyperparameter-loss
surfaces can be well approximated with a simple low rank model; the noisy quadratic. Empirically, this
approximation holds for a large region around the optimum. Since this region determines the practical
questions we care about, like tuning difficulty or the best attainable performance, we will use the noisy
quadratic throughout this study. For more details see Section A.2.
After drawing conclusions at the small scale, we still need the right hyperparameters to scale up. One strategy
parametrizes them so that their optimal values stay fixed across scales (Yang et al., 2021, 2024; Dey et al.,
2025; Mlodozeniec et al., 2026). A complementary approach fits scaling laws for the optimal values themselves
(DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b; Bergsma et al., 2025). Many methods work reasonably well. We
will later see that is is likely because as models grow, they become less sensitive to their hyperparameters
thus a configuration tuned cheaply at the small scale transfers well with just a little adjustment — a central
empirical finding in this study we will detail later.

2.3   Perplexity–Capability Correspondence
Scaling laws’ successes in pretraining inspired many to seek them for downstream tasks (Ivgi et al., 2022;
Gadre et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Mahmood et al., 2022; OpenAI, 2023). This stream of work delivered
mixed results however as downstream laws inherit and compound the tuning difficulties of upstream ones,
spread across multiple training stages and a battery of evaluations. As a consequence, downstream tasks
exhibit a wide range of unpredictable behavior, such as emergent (Wei et al., 2022; Srivastava et al., 2023),
inverse (McKenzie et al., 2023; Wilcox et al., 2024), or even U-shaped scaling (Wei et al., 2023). Lourie et al.
(2025b) found as little as 39% of tasks scale reliably. Carefully chosen metrics can mitigate some of this
irregularity (Schaeffer et al., 2023, 2025) but not eliminate it (Du et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026).
To sidestep this issue, researchers recast downstream performance as a function of pretraining loss instead of
data and compute (Xia et al., 2023; Snell et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Gadre et al., 2025;
Chen et al., 2025). Remarkably, Mayilvahanan et al. (2025) showed pretraining loss corresponds to capability
as long as the pretraining data is held fixed across changes in scale, hyperparameters, architecture, and even
the tokenizer. In other words: models of the same perplexity obtain the same capabilities, even when those
capabilities are hard to know in advance. We term this phenomenon perplexity–capability correspondence.
Hence in this study we will rely on and assume this correspondence; a practical illustration is in Appendix A.3.

What does it take to estimate scaling laws from small-scale experiments? We find them fairly robust to
different decisions (§3.1), but one ingredient makes or breaks the resulting law: hyperparameter tuning (§3.2).

3.1                        The Impact of Methodology
Despite the frequent debate over parameter definitions, we find they make little difference. Beyond that, we
study three refinements on a basic approach: tuning hyperparameters per token budget, decaying the learning
rate, and tying the scaling exponents. Some help but none, we will see, is what makes the estimate work.

Parameter definitions only make a small difference. When it comes to scaling laws, researchers define
parameters many different ways (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024). These
definitions differ along three axes: whether to account for attention, the embedding, and the unembedding
(Porian et al., 2024, §B). The disagreement pivots on the importance of parameters vs. compute: attention
uses more FLOPs on longer contexts, while the embedding layer uses no FLOPs at all. Figure 1 reveals these
differences in philosophy lead to little difference in outcome. We use the effective parameters (Attention and
Unembedding, no Embedding) as it corresponds most directly to FLOPs per token, keeping c = 6pd exact.

3.2                      The Importance of Hyperparameter Tuning
Figure 3 shows what happens with too little tuning effort. With 4 configurations per scale, the scaling law is
completely absent. With 16, the scaling law has yet to fully appear; it cannot be estimated reliably as the
suboptimal configurations inject too much noise. At 64, the law is clearly visible though extrapolation is
weak. Only at 256 configurations do we obtain an accurate scaling law. In our previous study, each refinement
improved precision on an already recognizable law; here, with too few configurations there is nothing to fit.
Thus one ingredient above all is required to estimate a scaling law, rigorous hyperparameter tuning. This fact
explains why small-scale laws are so easy to miss: uncovering them demands a search far more extensive than
most ever run. But why are small models so much more sensitive to their hyperparameters? We will show
this change in sensitivity comes from how the hyperparameter loss surface evolves as models scale.


4                   Understanding the Hyperparameter Loss Surface Across Scales
Throughout this section, we will make use of the same experiments we ran in the previous section. Note, that
we additionally fit a noisy quadratic distribution to each scale for our analysis. We show that models become
less sensitive to hyperparameters as they scale (§4.1). This phenomenon traces back to the hyperparameter
loss surface: larger scale makes it lower dimensional (§4.2). These facts influence how we design experiments
(§4.3): small scales require extensive search, while large scales are easy to adapt.

4.1                      Hyperparameter Tuning Gets Easier with Scale
With scale, good configurations become more common; thus, larger models trained longer are easier to tune.

Near-optimal configurations fill a larger fraction of the space. The effort required to find a good configuration
depends on the fraction of the space they take up. We can measure this volume via Monte Carlo integration:
sample configurations uniformly and check what percent land near the optimum. This procedure is exactly
random search. Figure 4(a,b) shows how the score distribution changes with scale: as models grow, the
distribution concentrates on the best achievable loss and the density forms a peak. Thus scale makes models
easier to tune.

Hyperparameter sensitivity decreases through both parameters and data. We show in Figure 4(d) that the
percentage of configurations within different loss thresholds of the optimal loss increases with scale for both
the ladder and random designs. Additionally, neither parameters nor data alone is responsible for this decrease
in sensitivity; the effect comes from both. In fact, Figure 16 shows the minimum, 10th, and 25th percentile
validation losses from random search and illustrates how sensitivity decreases across each. The gap from the
25th percentile to the minimum directly measures sensitivity because a small gap means that a quarter of all
draws are already close to optimal. Scaling one dimension while the other is held small lowers the loss and
slightly moves this gap, but scaling one while the other is large collapses it.

4.2             The Effective Number of Hyperparameters Decreases with Scale
Intuitively, a model with more hyperparameters is harder to tune; in practice, models can have many while
their effective number is still low. Formally, the effective number of hyperparameters (γ) is the surface’s
intrinsic dimension at the optimum, a major determinant of tuning difficulty (Lourie et al., 2025a). We
estimate γ at each parameter–data budget by fitting a noisy quadratic to the tail from random search (all
losses below a threshold, θ). We show these estimates against model size in Figure 4(d), where larger models
have fewer effective hyperparameters, dropping to one. Moreover, checkpoints across training largely share
the same intrinsic dimension, while increasing parameters clearly reduces γ.
Thus the effective number of hyperparameters shrinks as parameters grow. Data seems to have less effect.
Interestingly, prior work found the effective hyperparameters remains constant when using the same search
space across different architectures (Lourie et al., 2025a, §D). It seems counter-intuitive that γ depends on
scale but not architecture. Moreover, scale’s effect might change under an alternative parametrization, such
as maximum update (Yang et al., 2021). All these factors likely influence γ at some point, our results refine a
prior for typical regimes. Better understanding these limits would be a valuable direction for future work.

4.3             Implications for Small-Scale Experiments
The HP loss surface is not a black box; it has rich qualitative structure. We showed empirically that as
parameters and data grow, it gets easier to optimize, good configurations fill the space, and the intrinsic
dimension goes down. Good reason to hope that there is a simple structure underpinning the HP loss surface,
which we may exploit when optimizing at large scales. After all, (Lourie et al., 2025a) already showed the
noisy quadratic to be a good model for the outcomes of HP optimization at a single scale. Our efforts to model
the HP surface across scales however showed no consistent success. We describe several interpretable models

in §C (and tried many more). While simple models capture coarse features of the surface, none characterize
its finer details. The simplest model, one ignoring how HPs change with scale, is already a strong baseline.
This outcome agrees with the qualitative picture: extrapolating to larger scales, HPs have less impact and
little variation is left to model. As a result, we treat HP optimization independently per scale.


5     Designing Small-Scale Experiments
We synthesize our discussion into a methodology for small-scale experiments (§5.1). It emphasizes a holistic
view because while regularities exist across scales, extrapolation hits statistical limits (§5.2). We demonstrate
the approach on a case study from the literature: comparing pre-norm and post-norm transformers (§5.3).

5.1    A Methodology for Small-Scale Experiments
Our approach relies on three facts:
    1. with the pretraining data held fixed, capabilities depend on pretraining loss alone (§2.3),
    2. with rigorous hyperparameter tuning, scaling laws for pretraining extend to tiny scales (§3),
    3. and as models scale, they grow far less sensitive to their hyperparameters (§4).
These principles prescribe a methodology for model-centric research: tune and measure at the small scale,
understand how pretraining loss changes, then carry the winner up. The first fact says capability corresponds
to perplexity, so we can evaluate models by their cost to obtain it; the second says pretraining laws can be
observed at the small scale; and the third says the final scale-up is the easy part.
We expect these assumptions to hold, but should detect when they do not. The shape of small-scale experiments
lets us do just that: we can exploit the anticipated structure, check when it fails, and turn those failures into
useful diagnostics—for example, if either the noisy quadratic limit or scaling law fails to emerge then there is
likely an issue with the hyperparameters, model, or implementation.
More than checking assumptions, however, these diagnostics build a qualitative understanding of how the loss
surface scales. Such qualitative understanding is critical because quantitative approaches can compound small
errors, even when based on sound structure. More than pure extrapolation, a holistic approach is necessary.

5.3   Case Study: Pre-Norm vs. Post-Norm Architectures
Where should normalization layers go in a transformer? The original placed them after the residual connection
(post-norm) (Vaswani et al., 2017). This made training delicate, needing careful tuning. To stabilize training,
Baevski and Auli (2018) moved them before the sublayer (pre-norm). A year later, an influential empirical
study favored pre-norm in most settings yet, against today’s practice, found post-norm better for high-
resource machine translation (Nguyen and Salazar, 2019). The matter only really settled when Xiong et al.
(2020) showed theoretically that pre-norm improves gradients at initialization. Pre-norm’s advantage is most
pronounced in deeper models, whose experiments are more expensive. Resolving it took nearly three years
and a dedicated theoretical analysis. We will recover the answer from straightforward small-scale experiments.
A few well-separated scales should be far more informative than many nearby ones; we use three: two to fit
the law, and one to validate it. For post-norm, we sample 511 configurations at 4M, 512 at 34M, and 128 at
134M; for pre-norm, we sample 128 at 4M, 34M, and 134M. We fit on the smaller two then evaluate on the
largest one. This search is affordable because when parameters and data scale at the same rate compute is
quadratic in model size. A single 1B-parameter run costs as much as 64 runs at 134M, 1,024 runs at 34M, or
65,536 runs at 4M. Thus, our experiment costs as much as a handful of billion parameter runs.
We now deepen our understanding of each architecture through a sequence of qualitative analyses. Each
analysis is a diagnostic that asks a question and explores the structure we expect to find.

Diagnostic 1: have we tuned thoroughly? If random search approaches the optimum, then the score distribution
converges to a noisy quadratic as discussed in Section 2.2 and established by Lourie et al. (2025a). When the
limit fails, it is likely the search missed the optimum. We visualize the limit for each architecture in Figure 17.
For pre-norm it emerges readily; for post-norm it emerges too, but only deeper into the tail. This smaller
asymptotic regime is an early indication of increased hyperparameter sensitivity. We seem to have located the
optimum, but the tuning difficulty is itself our first finding: post-norm is sensitive to its hyperparameters, as
the literature suggests.

Diagnostic 2: will scaling up be easy? If larger models fail to become less sensitive to their hyperparameters,
then transferring from small to large scales might fail as well. Therefore, we examine how sensitivity scales for
the architectures at hand. Figure 18 compares the score distribution across scales for each. Both distributions
move in the right direction, shifting mass towards the minimum as good configurations fill more of the space.
For pre-norm the change is pronounced, growing a distinct peak at the optimum; for post-norm it is only
slight, as a second peak lingers at a suboptimal value. Post-norm again exhibits tuning difficulty.

Diagnostic 3: does perplexity still track capabilities? To evaluate by pretraining loss, it must correspond to
capabilities (§2.3). This perplexity–capability correspondence should hold when pretraining data is fixed; still,
the claim is easy to test as evaluating on a few tasks is much cheaper than pretraining the model. These
tasks must show signal at small scales, like AI2 ARC (Easy) (Clark et al., 2018). Figure 19 plots downstream
accuracy vs. pretraining loss for several such tasks. Regardless of the architecture, all points fall on a common
trend. Equal loss implies similar capabilities and a better pretraining law yields a more cost-effective model.

Diagnostic 4: do the scaling laws emerge? To estimate the scaling laws, we adopt improvements from §3.1:
tuning the hyperparameters per parameter–data budget and decaying the learning rate. Since extrapolation
primarily reflects the noisy irreducible error (§5.2), we try fitting the laws jointly with tied irreducible error,
as well as leaving them free to differ. Figure 20 validates these scaling laws on the held-out scale. The free vs.
joint laws extrapolate comparably. Pre-norm exhibits an excellent law, while post-norm’s law is reasonable
but could use improvement from further hyperparameter tuning. Again, hyperparameters present a challenge.
With a better understanding of each model, we now pool all sizes together and compare how the architectures
scale. Figure 21 presents the result: pre-norm scales better over the range of sizes we explore. Remembering
the statistical difficulty of extrapolating the scaling law (§5.2), we consider two kinds of comparisons. If we let
each scaling have its own irreducible error, then the post-norm architecture appears to eventually outperform
(beyond the scales we tested); however, if we share the irreducible error between the laws then the pre-norm
architecture remains more efficient.


                                                         8
From a holistic viewpoint, post-norm was harder to tune at every turn. Even a better architecture in theory
is worse in practice if we cannot find good hyperparameters. Beyond that, pre-norm scales better where the
scaling laws are most reliable: near the data. Finally, when we assume both architectures reach the same final
loss, pre-norm maintains its scaling advantage. These observations support pre-norm as the better choice.


6    Conclusion
Scaling laws promised experiments would stay cheap; however, this promise has gone unfulfilled. Today,
many believe regularity only emerges at large scales, and conclusions drawn at small scales will not transfer.
This misconception stems from a simple, unappreciated fact: small models are much more sensitive to their
hyperparameters. While scaling laws extend to very small sizes, they only emerge on the fully tuned frontier.
Reaching it requires an extensive search that researchers rarely conduct. Scaling laws predict pretraining loss,
and better loss does not always mean better capability; however, loss also fully captures capability—as long
as the data is held fixed. Therefore, a researcher who seeks not to predict capabilities but only to improve a
model can rely on loss since a model with a better law buys every loss, and thus any capability, at lower cost.
These insights suggest a new methodology of small-scale experiments, one that exploits the strong assumptions
we expect to hold true while checking when they do not. Working at small scales makes this rigor affordable: we
can train hundreds of models then use statistical tools, like the noisy quadratic limit, to test our assumptions.
Small-scale experiments save compute, but more than that they make our conclusions more robust.
Nevertheless, small-scale experiments have several limitations. While scaling laws exist at small scales, their
extrapolation hits statistical challenges. The small scale is informative, but requires judgment. Looking at the
bigger picture, is the model hard to tune? Is it getting easier? How does scaling compare where we trust
the law most? Instead of a single test, we should explore each of these questions using different diagnostics.
Perhaps the most important limitation of all, though, is the restriction to model-centric research. Changing the
data breaks the perplexity–capability correspondence which pretraining loss requires to proxy for downstream
tasks. With neither pretraining loss as a proxy nor reliable downstream scaling laws, small-scale experiments
have limited ability to predict large-scale outcomes. Hopefully future work will unlock small-scale experiments
for data-centric research; still, with care we can use small-scale experiments for model-centric research today.

A.1     Compute Scaling
Equation (1) omits the hyperparameters, describing loss at the fully tuned frontier : the best attainable loss
given the parameters and data ; however, parameters and data do not vary independently. The model’s
gradients must be computed for every example, so their product determines the total compute: c ∝ pd. Given
a budget, you face a trade-off between larger models and longer training. Navigating this trade-off is exactly
why people turn to scaling laws. Minimizing Equation (1) with fixed compute yields the condition:

                                                             ζ     η
                                                            ι ι =κ κ                                                           (2)
                                                             p    d
Hence the optimal parameters and data become power laws in compute:
                                                            κ                  ι
                                                    p ∝ c ι+κ        d ∝ c ι+κ                                                 (3)

If you plug these values into the joint scaling law then you obtain excess loss as a power law in the compute:
                                                                          ικ
                                                      L(p, d) − ϵ ∝ c− ι+κ                                                     (4)

Thus scaling laws reveal the best loss given the compute and how to get it by balancing parameters and data.
Hoffmann et al. (2022) found balanced parameters and data grow at the same rate, a conclusion since echoed
by many others (Anil et al., 2023; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024). This idea justifies the token-to-parameter ratio
as a meaningful measure of how well-trained a model is, with the compute-optimal ratio serving as baseline.
Under Equation (2), this same-rate growth only occurs when the scaling exponents are equal: ι = κ. Some
theoretical work predicts this equality (Bahri et al., 2024), and the estimates are often close in practice.
Researchers have also explored enforcing it to improve estimation (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025c).

A.2     The Noisy Quadratic Limit
How do we know if the hyperparameters are fully tuned, or how hard that is to accomplish? The noisy
quadratic limit answers these questions. Lourie et al. (2025a) found that near the optima, loss surfaces are:
    1. Quadratic: well approximated by the second-order Taylor expansion;
    2. Low-Rank: governed by a few directions that explain most of the variation; and
    3. Normal: distributed about the mean as additive normal noise.
Empirically, this approximation holds for a large region around the optimum, the asymptotic regime. This region
determines the practical questions we care about, like tuning difficulty or the best attainable performance.
Formally, if X are the hyperparameters, Y the validation score, and (x∗ , y∗ ) the optimum, then the conditional
distribution Y | X is approximately a quadratic plus additive normal noise, E.2 Letting Hx∗ be the Hessian:

B     Experimental Design
Our experiments explore the hyperparameter loss surface across scales via random search.

Models. We measure model size by the effective parameter count, parameters adjusted for FLOPs, so that
compute is c = 6pd for p effective parameters and d tokens. This count excludes the embedding layer (which
uses no FLOPs) but includes the unembedding layer and the cost of attention (Porian et al., 2024, §B). We
target parameter counts at increasing powers of two: 222 ≈ 4M to 228 ≈ 268M, and construct models at each
scale with two experimental designs: a hand-crafted model ladder and randomly sampled architectures.

Training and evaluation. We train with a warmup-stable-decay (WSD) schedule (Hu et al., 2024). Following
Hägele et al. (2024), we reuse the stable phase from the main run and branch off decay phases from checkpoints
taken 1/8, 2/8, . . . , 8/8 through training. We evaluate checkpoints before and after decaying the learning rate,
thus a single sweep yields stable and decayed evaluations across eight token budgets at a fraction of the cost.

Analysis. We partition the scales into training (4M–34M), validation (67M–134M), and test (268M), so
reported numbers measure extrapolation from small models to larger held-out ones. By default, we fit the
joint scaling law of Equation 1 to the best loss attained at each parameter–data budget pair. Like prior work
(Hilton et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025c), we found shorter training runs fit the scaling law poorly, therefore we
discard the two smallest token budgets which noticeably improves performance on our validation set.

B.1   Modeling
The modeling recipe we study is a Llama variant, as implemented in Meta Lingua: https://github.com/
facebookresearch/lingua (commit: 437d680) (Videau et al., 2024). Hyperparameters not specified here were
either left to Lingua’s defaults or swept over as described in Section B.2.

Data. We pretrain on the 100B token subset of FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., 2024). We use the subset to
save storage and preprocessing since the scales we consider require less than 100B tokens. We split this subset
into training and validation sets using Lingua’s built-in preprocessing pipeline with 48GB of memory and
Lingua’s suggested seed of 42. We create two such datasets: one shuffling all the data in 1 chunk for single
GPU training (4M–134M parameters), and another splitting the data into 8 chunks for 8 GPU training (268M
parameters).

Architecture. For the tokenizer, we use p50k_base from tiktoken (https://github.com/openai/tiktoken),
the BPE tokenizer from GPT-3.5 (text-davinci-003), because it has an appropriately sized vocabulary for our
scale. The context length is 1,024 with special tokens to distinguish the beginning and ending of each sequence.
For the neural network, we use a decoder-only Llama model (Grattafiori et al., 2024) available in Lingua
(apps/main/train.py) (Videau et al., 2024). The model uses RMSNorm for normalization (Zhang and Sennrich,
2019), SwiGLU for the activation function (Shazeer, 2020), and RoPE for position embeddings (Su et al.,
2024). All runs use a pre-normalization architecture except for those explicitly specified as post-normalization
(e.g., in §5), which we implemented as described by Xiong et al. (2020). Aside from Lingua’s defaults, we
padded the vocabulary size (model.vocab_size) to 50,536. In addition, we set model.multiple_of to 8 since
the default value of 256 is larger than our smaller models’ hidden dimensions; 8 compromises between allowing

B.2    Search Distributions
Our results analyze three different experiments, each defined by a search distribution over the hyperparameters.
All the experiments randomly sample configurations across a range of scales, with each scale defined by the
effective number of parameters. The effective number of parameters is proportional to the FLOPs per token
so that c = 6pd when c is compute, p the effective parameters, and d the number of tokens. Following Porian
et al. (2024, §B), we compute it as:

                                         Nef f := (3df f + 4d) dl + dv + ndl                                        (10)

where df f is the feedforward dimension, d the hidden dimension, l the number of layers, v the vocabulary size,
and n the context length of the transformer. In particular, parameters in the embedding layer are not counted
because they do not contribute FLOPs, while parameters in the unembedding layer are counted because they
do. See Porian et al. (2024, §B) for an overview of different ways to count parameters.

8 when running on a GPU, and so on). These constraints must be considered when designing a ladder.

Random Architecture (random). We sample configurations for target scales starting from Nef f = 222 ≈ 4M
up to 227 ≈ 134M in powers of two. Excluding 2 failed runs, we search 128 configurations at 4M effective
parameters, 126 at 8M, 128 at 17M, 128 at 34M, 128 at 67M, and 128 at 134M. In the random experiment,
we also tune the architectural hyperparameters via random search. The number of layers and attention heads
for each configuration are sampled from the distribution described in Figure 9. Given the effective parameters,
the number of layers determines the hidden dimension via Equation (10), which is a quadratic in d given the
other parameters and the fact that df f = 8/3d for our model. We round the solution to the nearest multiple
of 8 for GPU efficiency. Once we have the hidden dimension, the number of heads determines the head
dimension: dim = head_dim × n_heads. We ensure n_layers is at least 2 because induction heads cannot
form with fewer layers (Olsson et al., 2022); we also use a square root scale because we found it balanced


                                                        25
making both the number of layers and the hidden dimension more uniform. n_heads must divide the hidden
dimension, so we sample proper divisors of dim/8 to guarantee that head_dim is even (required by RoPE) and
greater than 16 (required by the attention implementation). Since small divisors are much more common,
we bias towards larger ones by sampling divisors with probability proportional to their size. As previously
mentioned, transformers are subject to a number of constraints that must be accounted for when sampling
architectural hyperparameters: head_dim ≥ 16 for common attention implementations, head_dim must be
even for RoPE, head_dim divides dim, dim provides both the hidden and the embedding dimension, and
matrix dimensions should be multiples of 8 for GPU efficiency. These constraints complicate designing a good
search distribution;3 the distribution in Figure 9 has been constructed to obey them.

Pre-Normalization (prenorm) vs. Post-Normalization (postnorm). We sample configurations for target
scales of Nef f = 222 ≈ 4M , 225 ≈ 34M , and 227 ≈ 134M . For prenorm, we reuse all the results at those scales
from the random experiment. For postnorm, we reuse the initial configurations of the random experiment
(including architectural parameters), but we re-run them after changing the transformer to a post-normalization
architecture. In addition, we expand the number of configurations due to the post-normalization architecture’s
increased tuning difficulty. Excluding 1 failed run, this expansion results in 511 configurations at 4M effective
parameters, 512 at 34M, and 128 at 134M.

B.3   Analysis
Noisy Quadratic Limit. We generate confidence bands for the eCDF using the LD Highest Density bands
from Lourie et al. (2024), and we fit the noisy quadratic distribution as described in Lourie et al. (2025a),
both using opda (v0.8.0): https://github.com/nicholaslourie/opda. To fit the noisy quadratic limit, we
set the threshold θ at various percentiles of the score distribution. We used different percentiles for each
parameter–data budget, with the size of the asymptotic regime generally increasing in both. In the ladder
and random / prenorm) experiments, they were as follows: for 4M, 8M, and 17M effective parameters, 7% at
checkpoints 1–6 and 8% at checkpoints 7–8; for 34M, 7% for checkpoints 1–2, 8% at 3, 10% at 4–6, and 12%
at 7–8; for 67M, 12% at 1, 15% at 2, 20% at 3, 25% at 4, 30% at 5, 32% at 6, and 35% at 7–8; for 134M and
268M, 12% at 1, 15% at 2, 20% at 3, 25% at 4, 30% at 5, 35% at 6, 40% at 7, and 50% at 8. In the postnorm
experiment, they were as follows: for 4M effective parameters, 3% at checkpoint 1, 4% at 2, 5% at 3, 6% at 4,
and 7% at 5–8; for 34M and 134M, 7% at 1–2, 8% at 3, and 10% at 4–8. We restricted α to be positive (as
bits-per-character always is), and we restricted γ to be 1 to at most the nominal number of hyperparameters
(7 for ladder and 9 for random / prenorm and postnorm).

Scaling Laws. To fit and evaluate the scaling laws, we partition the scales into training (4M–34M), validation
(67M–134M), and testing (268M); thus, we evaluate the scaling laws based on their ability to extrapolate
from small to large models. We used training to fit the laws, validation to make decisions during our research,
and testing for the final evaluation. For the scaling law, we fit Equation (1) to the best validation loss
from each parameter–data budget pair, or (where explicitly stated) to the validation losses from the run
achieving the minimum final loss at each scale. In both cases, we drop the first two checkpoints (1/8 and 2/8 of
training) as it substantially improved the fit (Hilton et al., 2023). Where specified, we also tie the scaling
exponents together (ι = κ). To perform the optimization, we minimized the mean squared error via SciPy’s
scipy.optimize.differential_evolution (Virtanen et al., 2020), with bounds of [0, 1] for ϵ, [0, 10] for ln ζ and
ln η (which we fit on log scales), and [0, 1] for ι and κ. We used a popsize of 120 except when fitting laws
jointly with a single irreducible error for which we used a popsize of 60 due to the higher dimensionality
and longer runtime. To evaluate the scaling laws, we report mean squared error between the predicted and
observed loss on the held-out scales, with held-out scales using the same preprocessing as those in training
(e.g., whether the best run or the best configuration for each parameter–data budget is used).

Interpretable Models of the Hyperparameter Loss Surface. In Section C, we compare various models of the
hyperparameter loss surface. These models are fit to losses generated via the same methodology used for
scaling laws (best loss for each parameter–data budget, dropping the first two checkpoints), except instead of
selecting the loss for the single best configuration, we take all configurations within the asymptotic regime

B.4    Implementation
Our implementation further involves the following details.4

Training Pipeline. We use gradient accumulation so that we can tune the batch size despite different scales
requiring different amounts of memory and GPU setups (1 vs. 8 GPUs). We set the per-GPU batch size to
the largest power of two that safely avoids out-of-memory errors in order to maximize throughput while also
evenly dividing the number of tokens (16 sequences for 4M–67M parameters, 8 sequences for 134M–268M
parameters). Each scale is assigned a base number of gradient accumulation steps chosen so that after
accounting for the per-GPU batch size and the number of GPUs the model will process 64 sequences per
update. We then multiply that base by a power of two to achieve the distribution described in Figure 8 for
the total batch size. As a result, the batch size sweep is hardware-agnostic and always evenly divides the
total number of training tokens. In addition, we set gc_collect_freq to 1,024, data.prefetch_size to 1,024,
distributed.compile to true, and distributed.fsdp_type to "full_shard" in order to make training efficient.
Finally, we set logging.freq to 1 and found it necessary to set env.ENABLE_INTRA_NODE_COMM to
"0" to avoid an unresolved error in the PyTorch version.

Software. We train the models using Meta Lingua (commit: 437d680), as previously described (Videau et al.,
2024). We modify the code base to support the 100B token subset of FineWeb-Edu, use a linear decay in the
WSD schedule, and use PyTorch’s Scaled Dot Product Attention (SDPA) instead of Flex Attention as Flex
Attention would not successfully generate kernels for some of the transformer shapes that we randomly sample.
In addition, we switch the transformer architecture from pre-norm to post-norm for some experiments as
previously discussed. We isolate the software environment in an Apptainer (Singularity) container (Developers,
2021). The container runs Ubuntu 24.04.2 LTS with CUDA 12.8 (V12.8.93) and cuDNN 9.8, and we run
Lingua via a Python 3.11.14 conda environment with PyTorch 2.7.0+cu128 (Paszke et al., 2019).

Hardware. Training was done on 80GB NVIDIA A100 GPUs on a SLURM cluster. Model sizes from
4M–134M effective parameters were trained with 1 GPU, while models with 268M effective parameters were
trained data-parallel with 8 GPUs.

C    Interpretable Models of the Hyperparameter Loss Surface
In Section 4.3, we discussed developing an interpretable model of the hyperparameter loss surface across scales
by combining the noisy quadratic limit with scaling laws. We reported that such models capture the broad
shape of the surface, but not precisely how it evolves. Here we provide those explorations in more depth. In
particular, we will compare several approaches a researcher might be tempted to try and show that while they
have some success in modeling the loss surface, they are far from the clear wins available via scaling laws or
the noisy quadratic limit on their own.
Scaling laws model the optimal loss across scales; the noisy quadratic limit models how hyperparameters
influence the loss at one scale. To combine them, we must set the quadratic’s minimum according to the
scaling law. One could accomplish this in several ways;5 a simple approach with a strong intuition starts from
the observation that, given any reasonable hyperparameters, the loss should approach the irreducible error (ϵ)
as parameters and data go to infinity. We capture this by modeling the percent increase in reducible loss at
each scale as a quadratic in the hyperparameters:

D.1   Scaling law error propagation
Scaling laws are fitted to losses measured at a handful of scales and then extrapolated to a larger ones. Each
measured loss, however, carries error from a finite tuning budget (section D.2), random seeds, or estimation
noise. If the irreducible error were known, this problem would reduce to fitting a power law: essentially a
linear regression in log-log space. In practice, the irreducible error is not known, but the fitting procedure
can behave quite similarly. We ask a simple question: if the loss at one scale is off by ϵ, how much does that
corrupt the loss we extrapolate to at a larger scale? For the linear case, the answer is exact, closed form and
can be retrieved from standard literature on linear regression.
Throughout, we index the K scales by j = 1, . . . , K; each scale contributes one data point to the regression.

best-of-M score TM from a tuning run (section D.2), so that the measurement error ϵ at a scale can itself be
understood through the sampling analysis of the previous section. Figure 11 illustrates the whole construction.

For any ρ > 2 the larger scale receives fewer runs yet more compute than its neighbour: a handful of expensive

A manual for designing a compute-optimal scaling law estimate. Any practitioner should roughly follow the
following steps in designing their experiment:
   1. Estimate (or set) ρ, the relative cost of a scale Nj = N1 ρ j−1 . For transformers this cost is well
      approximated by the standard 6N D estimate (roughly six FLOPs per parameter per token over the
      forward and backward pass) (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), so once a practitioner chooses
      a model ladder and its token counts, every Nj , and hence ρ, is fixed. (Anyone building their own ladder
      has to do this FLOP accounting up front to know what a run at each scale will cost.)
   2. Assume the effective dimensions γj . We take these as known. This is not a perfect assumption, but it is
      broadly understood where along a model ladder tuning becomes reliable and γ drops, and the choice
      can be checked after the fact from the realized slope of error against compute at each scale.
   3. Compute the total budget C. This is normally known outright usually in GPU-hours, converted to
      FLOPs through the hardware’s sustained throughput. The only remaining factor, the per-scale error
      amplitude Aj , varies slowly and is typically taken comparable across scales (or read off the loss spread if
      not).
With ρ, the γj , and C in hand, the number of runs per scale can be computed. The multiplierPλ is computed
through Equation (31), and it is set by substituting the Mj⋆ into the budget constraint j Mj Nj = C
(Equation (26)) and solving the resulting equation for λ. Reading the Mj⋆ back out of Equation (31) then
gives the number of tuning runs to launch at each scale.
